Sometimes a section needs something the standard cycle does not provide: a
competency assessed that is not on the form, a one-off evaluation for somebody
whose situation does not fit the calendar, or a score revisited because the
scorecard was written before the work changed.

Before this screen existed, all three happened by message — and the only
surviving record was the change itself, with nobody's name against the reason
for it. Now you ask here, and the answer is kept with the question.

## The three things you can ask for

| Request | Use it when |
|---|---|
| **Extra competency** | Something your section is assessed on is missing from the form. You can ask for one person or for the whole unit. |
| **Special evaluation** | Somebody needs an evaluation outside the normal schedule — a mid-cycle move, a secondment, a probation that does not line up with the calendar. |
| **Scoring adjustment** | A score is wrong for a reason that is explainable, not arguable: the scorecard predates the work, or a target was reassigned mid-period. |

## How it goes

:::diagram request-flow

:::steps
- You raise it — choosing the type, who it is about, and why. The reason is required.
- HCM is told — everybody who can decide it gets a notification; it does not sit in a queue nobody is watching.
- They approve or decline — a decline must say why, so you are not left guessing at a resubmission.
- Everyone sees the record — who asked, who answered, when, and on what grounds.
:::

## What an approval does — and does not do

:::warning
**Approving a request records a decision. It does not make the change.**

Nobody's score moves, no competency is added and no evaluation opens because a
request was approved. Somebody still makes the change on the screen that owns
it.
:::

This is deliberate, and it is worth understanding rather than working around.
If an approval wrote the new score itself, the history would show a request, an
approval, and then a different number — with nothing saying who changed it, on
which screen, against which version of the form. "Who changed this score" has to
have an answer.

There is a practical reason too. Approvals are often conditional: *yes, but only
for the branch staff*; *yes, once the cycle reopens*; *yes at 3.6 rather than
3.8*. A flow that applied the request exactly as written would have nowhere to
put any of that, so the approver would end up declining something they meant to
allow.

## Rules worth knowing before you are surprised by them

- **You cannot raise a request about yourself.** Not an extra competency on your
  own record, not a special evaluation of yourself, and certainly not a lift to
  your own score.
- **You cannot decide your own request**, even if you hold the permission to
  decide others. Two gates that one person can pass alone are not two gates.
- **Withdrawing is not the same as being declined.** If you take a request back,
  it is recorded as withdrawn. "How many of my requests were refused" is a
  question worth being able to answer honestly.
- **A decision is final.** A decided request cannot be re-decided; raise a new
  one if circumstances have changed, and the pair will read as the sequence it
  was.

## Who can see a request

The person who raised it, and anybody who can decide it.

:::note
**The subject of a request does not see it.** A request to adjust somebody's
score is a judgement still being formed, in the same category as a draft
evaluation or an unreleased review. What they are entitled to is the result,
once somebody applies it — not the argument about whether to.
:::
